Erdős, Algebraic Number Theory, and the Power of Parallel Search

Filed under: AI cognition, Erdős, Kolmogorov, creativity

How did AI disprove a 1946 conjecture that had stumped combinatorial geometers for 80 years? Not by being smarter than Erdős, but by being tireless — it tried thousands of obscure constructions from algebraic number theory without fatigue or discouragement.

The Erdős unit distance conjecture posited that the maximum number of unit-distance pairs among n points in the plane is achieved by a regular square grid. For 80 years, mathematicians tried to prove it. The grid seemed optimal. The intuition was strong.

But AI found something humans hadn't: a construction from algebraic number theory — a domain that combinatorial geometers rarely visit — that yields strictly more unit-distance pairs than the grid. The construction is not elegant in the traditional sense. It's not something a human mathematician would have tried, because it lives in a different part of the mathematical landscape.

The formal insight: AI's advantage is not intelligence in the human sense. It is the absence of psychological cost. A human mathematician cannot try construction #847 after constructions #1-846 have failed. An AI can. This is the operational substrate of the Mathocalypse.

When we formalize this with Kolmogorov complexity, the picture sharpens: AI discovered a low-Kolmogorov-complexity bridge between combinatorial geometry and algebraic number theory. The bridge was always there in the mathematical landscape. No human found it because the search space is vast and the psychological cost of failure is high.
