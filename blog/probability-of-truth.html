Probability · Statistics · Information Theory

The Probability of Truth

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read · Part 3 of 3 in the Mathematics with ML series

How do machines reason under uncertainty? The answer spans three mathematical traditions — probability theory, statistics, and information theory — that turn out to be three views of the same underlying structure. Every trained neural network is implicitly computing a posterior. Every compression algorithm is fighting entropy. Every claim about intelligence reduces, ultimately, to the shortest description.

Bayes' Theorem: The Calculus of Belief

$$P(H|D) = \frac{P(D|H) \, P(H)}{P(D)}$$

The posterior $P(H|D)$ — our updated belief after seeing data $D$ — is proportional to the likelihood $P(D|H)$ times the prior $P(H)$. This single equation is the calculus of belief: every observation refines our understanding.

The prior encodes what we think is plausible before seeing data. It might be wide (admitting many possibilities) or narrow (reflecting strong assumptions). The prior is where domain knowledge enters Bayesian reasoning.

The likelihood measures how well the data fits each hypothesis. It is the evidence — what the data tells us, separate from what we believed before. Strong evidence reshapes our beliefs.

The posterior is the updated belief. In our interactive clip, you can adjust the prior strength $\alpha$ to see the posterior shift between the wide prior and the narrow likelihood. When $\alpha = 0$, the posterior equals the prior (we ignore data). When $\alpha = 1$, the posterior equals the likelihood (we ignore our prior). The truth is always in between.

Every Model Is Bayesian

Every ML model that outputs a probability is implicitly Bayesian. The training data is evidence, the parameters are hypotheses, and the learned distribution is the posterior. Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) is Bayes with a uniform prior. Maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation is Bayes with an explicit prior. Regularization is a prior. Dropout is a prior. Every design choice in deep learning is, implicitly, a statement about what we believed before.

Entropy: Uncertainty Quantified

$$H(X) = -\sum_x p(x) \log p(x)$$

Entropy quantifies uncertainty. A distribution that is certain (one outcome has probability 1) has zero entropy. A uniform distribution has maximum entropy. Entropy is the expected number of bits needed to encode an outcome — it connects probability to information.

Crucially, the Shannon entropy and the thermodynamic entropy are the same quantity. The formula $S = k_B \ln \Omega$ (Boltzmann) and $H = -\sum p \log p$ (Shannon) differ only by a constant. Every compression algorithm is fighting entropy. Every learning algorithm is reducing entropy — turning uncertain distributions into confident ones.

The Bekenstein Bound

The connection goes deeper. The Bekenstein-Hawking entropy of a black hole:

$$S_{BH} = \frac{A}{4L_P^2}$$

where $A$ is the horizon area and $\ell_P$ is the Planck length — this is the maximum entropy that can fit in a region of space. The universe itself has an entropy budget, and it's measured in bits. Information is physical.

The Information Bottleneck

Tishby and Zaslavsky (2015) proposed that deep learning is an information bottleneck: compress the input $X$ into a representation $Z$ while preserving information about the output $Y$:

$$\min_{p(Z|X)} I(X; Z) - \beta \, I(Z; Y)$$

The representation should contain everything about $Y$ and nothing else about $X$. This is the variational principle of deep learning — compression and prediction, balanced. This principle explains why deep networks generalize: a network that compresses has low $I(X; Z)$ and captures only the relevant structure — the structure that predicts $Y$. Generalization is compression.

Kolmogorov Complexity: The Ultimate Prior

$$K(x) = \min_{p: U(p) = x} |p|$$

The Kolmogorov complexity of a string $x$ is the length of the shortest program $p$ that produces $x$ on a universal Turing machine $U$. It is the ultimate measure of information content — the minimum number of bits needed to describe $x$, regardless of encoding.

But $K(x)$ is uncomputable — determining it would require solving the halting problem. We can never know the exact Kolmogorov complexity of anything.

AIXI: The Universal Intelligence

Hutter's AIXI defines universal intelligence as an agent that weights every computable program by $2^{-|p|}$ (Solomonoff induction):

$$a^* = \arg\max_a \sum_\mu 2^{-|\mu|} \mathbb{E}[V | a, \mu]$$

The shortest programs that explain the data are most likely. This is the mathematical ideal of intelligence — and it's uncomputable. Every neural network is a computable approximation of this universal prior.

The Unifying View

Probability, statistics, and information theory are not three separate fields — they are three views of the same structure:

Probability is the language of uncertainty.
Statistics is the practice of updating beliefs from data.
Information theory quantifies how much belief-updating is possible.

Bayes' theorem connects the first two. Entropy connects the second and third. Kolmogorov complexity connects all three to computability. And machine learning sits at the intersection — a practical art built on a mathematical foundation that runs all the way down to the physics of information.
