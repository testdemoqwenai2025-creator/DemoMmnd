The Convergence

Prime numbers, formal proof, and machine intelligence are not three fields. They are one field, seen from three angles.

This page braids the threads. 1Primes, and the mystery of their distribution
The starting question · timeline: riemann thread

Every integer greater than 1 is a unique product of primes. This single fact — the fundamental theorem of arithmetic — makes primes the atoms of number theory. Yet their distribution among the integers is, even today, not fully understood. They thin out (the average gap near N grows like log N), but they do so erratically: there are long stretches with none, then pairs separated by only 2.

The function that counts them, π(x) = #{primes ≤ x}, is a staircase. It jumps by 1 at each prime and is flat between. In 1859, in a nine-page memoir, Bernhard Riemann proposed that the secret of this staircase lies in a single complex function: the zeta function ζ(s). Every jagged step of π(x), Riemann suggested, is the visible trace of the zeros of ζ.

$$\pi(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\mu(n)}{n} \, \mathrm{Li}(x^{1/n}) \quad \text{(Riemann's explicit formula, 1859)}$$

You can watch the staircase climb against the smooth logarithmic integral Li(x) in Playground 2. The gap you see — the prime mystery — is what every subsequent stage of the convergence tries to close.

2Zeta, and the line where the zeros must lie
Riemann's conjecture · the load-bearing assertion

The zeta function ζ(s) = Σ n−s converges for Re(s) > 1, but its analytic continuation is defined everywhere except s = 1. The non-trivial zeros — the ones that matter for primes — all lie in the critical strip 0 < Re(s) < 1. Riemann computed the first several and found them all on the single line Re(s) = 1/2. He conjectured, without proof, that all non-trivial zeros lie there.

This is the Riemann Hypothesis: the most important open problem in mathematics, a Hilbert problem (1900), a Clay Millennium problem (2000), and the load-bearing assertion behind the prime-counting error term. Under RH, the error in π(x) ~ Li(x) is O(√x log x); without it, the best bound is exponentially weaker. The exponent 1/2 — the square root — is the entire content of RH.

The exponent ½ in √x is RH. Any larger exponent would be a weaker statement that does not imply RH; any smaller would be false.— the √X theta-lift chain, card 16

In Playground 1 you can scrub along the critical line and watch ζ(½+it) trace a parametric curve that crosses the origin at every Riemann zero (the first ten are tabulated: 14.13, 21.02, 25.01, …). In Playground 3 the same √x appears as the gold band around the von Mangoldt staircase ψ(x) — the visible RH envelope that the staircase never escapes.

3Formal proof, and the computer as the most powerful prover on Earth
From Riemann's pencil to Lean's kernel · timeline: formal thread

A proof is only as good as the trust we place in its steps. For three centuries, mathematical trust was social: peer review, reputational, fallible. In 2013 Lean changed the substrate. A Lean proof is a program that type-checks against a small, audited kernel; if it compiles, it is correct. The community library Mathlib has since grown past 1.5 million lines of formalized mathematics — the largest corpus of machine-checked math ever assembled.

In March 2025, Mathlib formalized a Riemann zeta zero-free region extending to Re(s) ≥ 7/8 (with Im(s) ≠ 0). It is a machine-checked strengthening of the classical de la Vallée-Poussin bound — the first Lean proof in the chain that targets RH itself. And in July 2024, DeepMind's AlphaProof, built on Lean, solved four of six International Mathematical Olympiad problems — silver-medalist level, the first credible demonstration that an AI-augmented theorem prover can do competition mathematics.

$$\text{Lean formalizes: } \zeta(s) \neq 0 \text{ for } \mathrm{Re}(s) \geq \tfrac{7}{8},\ \mathrm{Im}(s) \neq 0 \quad \text{(Mathlib, 2025)}$$

This is the hinge of the convergence. The same formal machinery that checks Lean proofs is the substrate on which AI reasoners operate: if a machine can verify a proof, a machine can search for one. The road from Riemann's 1859 pencil sketch to a 2026 Lean-checked zero-free region runs straight into the next stage.

4LLM reasoners, and the machine that can hold a proof in mind
From verification to generation · timeline: ml thread

A formal proof system can check a proof, but discovering one is a search problem over a space so vast that brute force is hopeless. The transformer architecture — scaled dot-product attention, published in 2017 — gave machines the ability to hold context, to reason across thousands of tokens, to propose the next step of an argument the way a graduate student does. By 2024, AlphaProof was proposing Lean tactics that compiled. By 2026, the same attention mechanism underwrites every frontier LLM.

But raw next-token prediction is not enough. A reasoner that merely sounds right is dangerous in mathematics, where a single wrong step invalidates the whole. The deep insight is that the formal system provides the ground truth: an LLM can propose, Lean can check, and the loop can iterate. Löb's theorem (card 10) is the logical pivot — the formal condition under which a system can trust its own proofs. AIXI (card 12) is the theoretical ceiling: the optimal agent that combines Solomonoff induction with sequential decision theory.

Attention is all you need — but attention without verification is hallucination. The formal system is what turns a fluent speaker into a reliable mathematician.— the convergence, stage 4

And the loop is not just proof. The same LLM that reads Lean tactics can read information bottlenecks (card 9), compress Kolmogorov complexity (card 6), and — via Weight-Space Programming — directly read and write the weights of another network. The boundary between reasoning and surgery begins to blur.

5Reinforcement learning, and the shaping of every modern LLM
From consequence to alignment · timeline: ml thread

An LLM that proposes proofs is still passive: it waits to be prompted. Reinforcement learning closes the loop — the agent acts, observes a reward, and updates. The agent does not merely predict; it decides. The mathematical skeleton is the Bellman equation: a value function V that satisfies a fixed-point condition over the expected return.

$$V^{\pi}(s) = \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t r_{t+1} \,\middle|\, s_0 = s\right] \quad \text{(the RL fixed point)}$$

Since 2022 this is not abstract. RLHF — Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback — is the three-stage pipeline (supervised fine-tuning, reward model, PPO) that shaped ChatGPT and every frontier LLM since. The deep-dive §7 documents the recipe. The same loop, taken into physics, becomes HydroGym: differentiable fluid dynamics paired with Gradient-Enhanced PPO, controlling a Karman vortex street in real time, with zero-shot transfer from 2D training to 3D deployment.

Notice the recursion. The LLM that proposes Lean proofs (stage 4) is itself the product of RL (stage 5). The agent that learns to prove is shaped by reward. And the reward that shapes it can, in principle, be ‘did the proof compile?’ — the formal system (stage 3) supplying the ground truth that the RL loop (stage 5) optimizes against. The three stages are not parallel; they are a closed loop.

6SINDy, and the discovery of the equation from observation alone
From data to law · timeline: physics thread

The loop closes in physics. A dynamical system — a fluid, a pendulum, a climate — produces data. For centuries, the equation governing it was guessed by a genius (Navier, Stokes, Lorenz) and then checked against experiment. SINDy (Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics, Brunton-Proctor-Kutz, 2016) inverts this: given the trajectory data, find the sparsest combination of basis functions whose derivative matches the observed one. The equation is discovered, not guessed.

$$\dot{X} = \Theta(X)\,\Xi \quad \text{where } \Xi \text{ is sparse (SINDy, 2018)}$$

This is the final convergence. The primes (stage 1) posed the original question of structure beneath apparent chaos. The zeta function (stage 2) gave the analytic handle. Formal proof (stage 3) made verification mechanical. LLM reasoners (stage 4) made search fluent. Reinforcement learning (stage 5) made the agent goal-directed. And SINDy (stage 6) is the same principle applied to physics: find the sparse, verifiable law that the data implies. The Schrödinger equation (card 3), the Hamiltonian (deep-dive §4), the adjoint method (deep-dive §5) — each is a case where the governing equation can now be recovered from observation by a machine, checked for consistency, and refined by an RL-trained agent that gets reward for prediction accuracy.

The same shape — sparse structure recovered from data, verified against ground truth, refined by consequence — runs from Riemann's explicit formula to SINDy's library. The convergence is not an analogy; it is a single method, seen at six scales.— the convergence, closing
